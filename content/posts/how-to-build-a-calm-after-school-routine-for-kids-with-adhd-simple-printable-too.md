## Why After-School Transitions Are Tough  

Kids with ADHD often experience a surge of sensory input and emotional energy during the school day. When the bell rings, that energy doesn't just switch off; it needs a outlet. Without a predictable structure, the sudden shift from a structured classroom to a less-defined home environment can feel overwhelming, leading to:  

- **Emotional overload** - frustration, tears, or anger that seems to come out of nowhere.  
- **Homework resistance** - the brain is still "on" from school, making it hard to sit down for tasks.  
- **Meltdowns** - a loss of control that can leave both child and parent exhausted.  

A visual after-school routine gives the child a concrete roadmap, signaling what comes next and reducing the anxiety of the unknown. It also provides parents with a reliable framework to guide the evening, cutting down on repeated reminders and power struggles.

## The Power of Visual Routines  

Visual schedules work because they tap into the strengths many kids with ADHD already have: they respond well to clear, concrete cues rather than abstract verbal instructions. When a child can see a picture of "snack time" followed by "homework," the brain can anticipate the sequence, which:  

- **Reduces anxiety** - predictability lowers the stress response.  
- **Builds independence** - the child learns to check the chart and move forward without constant prompting.  
- **Supports executive functioning** - sequencing, time management, and task initiation are practiced in a low-stakes setting.  

The printable toolkit includes ready-made icons for common after-school activities (snack, play, chores, homework, calm-down, bedtime prep). You can customize the order to fit your family's unique schedule.

## Step-by-Step: Building Your After-School Routine  

Follow these numbered steps to create a routine that feels natural, not forced. Adjust the timing based on your child's age, school dismissal time, and any extracurricular commitments.  

1. **Observe the Current Flow (5-10 minutes)**  
   - Spend one afternoon simply noting what happens from the moment your child walks in the door until bedtime.  
   - Write down the sequence: e.g., "door   backpack drop   snack   TV   homework   dinner   bath   story   lights out."  
   - Identify where things tend to fall apart (e.g., after snack, before homework).  

2. **Choose 3-4 Core Activities**  
   - From your observation, pick the essential blocks that must happen each day (snack, homework, chores, calm-down).  
   - Keep the list short; too many steps can feel overwhelming.  

3. **Select Visual Cues**  
   - Open the printable toolkit and find icons that match each core activity (e.g., an apple for snack, a pencil for homework, a broom for chores, a bubble-bath for calm-down).  
   - Print the icons on cardstock, cut them out, and laminate if possible for durability.  

4. **Create the Schedule Strip**  
   - Use a long piece of poster board, a magnetic strip, or a simple clipboard.  
   - Attach the icons in the order you want them to occur, leaving a small space between each for a check-off.  
   - Add a movable marker (a clothespin, a magnet, or a sticky-note arrow) that your child can slide to the next icon as they complete each step.  

5. **Set Clear Time Buffers (Optional but Helpful)**  
   - If your child struggles with transitions, add a 2-minute "transition buffer" icon (a simple hourglass or timer) between major blocks.  
   - This signals that it's okay to pause, take a deep breath, or stretch before moving on.  

6. **Launch the Routine Together**  
   - Explain the chart in a calm, upbeat tone: "This is our new after-school map. When you finish something, you move the arrow to the next picture."  
   - Do a quick run-through together, letting your child move the marker as you narrate each step.  
   - Praise the effort, not just the outcome: "I love how you checked the chart and moved to homework all by yourself!"  

7. **Review and Tweak Weekly**  
   - At the end of each week, spend two minutes chatting about what worked and what felt off.  
   - Swap out an icon, adjust the order, or add a new activity (like a short outdoor play break) based on feedback.

## Choosing Calm-Down Activities  

Even with a solid routine, moments of overwhelm can pop up. Having a handful of quick, sensory-friendly calm-down options ready can prevent a small frustration from snowballing into a meltdown. The toolkit includes printable cards for activities such as:  

- **Deep-breathing shapes** (trace a square while inhaling for 4 counts, exhaling for 4).  
- **Pressure-point squeezes** (a stress ball or a folded towel to press gently).  
- **Quiet-corner prompts** (a small tent, a bean bag, or a favorite blanket with a picture of a calm scene).  
- **Movement breaks** (jumping jacks, toe touches, or a quick walk to the mailbox).
