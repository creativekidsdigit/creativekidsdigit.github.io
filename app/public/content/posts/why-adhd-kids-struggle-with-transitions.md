## A. Teacher-Friendly Explanation (~1,500 words)

### Why Your ADHD Students Melt Down Between Activities - And What's Really Going On

If you teach, you already know the moment: the lesson was going fine, and then you said "okay, put that away and get out your math books" - and suddenly one student is under the desk, another is arguing, and a third just... freezes. It's not defiance. It's not laziness. And it's not really about math, either. It's about what a transition actually asks of a brain.

**What a transition really is**

We tend to picture "transitions" as the big, obvious ones - walking to the gym, packing up at the end of the day. But research on classroom time shows that transitions happen constantly, and many are invisible to us as adults: switching from listening to writing, changing the pace of an activity, shifting expectations mid-lesson. Every one of those small shifts is a transition, and studies that directly observe classrooms find transition time can eat up anywhere from 15% to over 30% of a class period. That's not a small thing. It's real instructional time, and it's exactly where ADHD students struggle most.

**Why ADHD makes this so much harder**

A landmark meta-analysis pooling 83 studies and nearly 6,700 children (Willcutt et al., 2005) found that kids with ADHD show measurable, consistent weaknesses in several mental skills - and these aren't explained by lower intelligence, weaker academics, or "just needing to try harder." The skills involved are:

- **Working memory** - holding new instructions in mind while letting go of the old task.
- **Inhibition** - stopping an ongoing behavior on cue, even when it's fun.
- **Cognitive flexibility (set-shifting)** - mentally switching gears from one kind of thinking to another.
- **Planning** - sequencing the steps needed to get from here to there.
- **Emotional regulation** - managing the frustration or disappointment that comes with change.

Here's the part that matters most for your classroom: a transition asks for *all five of these at once*. Sustained tasks - like reading quietly for 20 minutes - mostly ask for one or two of these skills. A transition stacks them. That's why a student who seems totally fine during independent work can fall apart the second you say "switch."

**What's happening in the brain**

Brain imaging research backs this up. A meta-analysis of 55 brain-imaging studies (Cortese et al., 2012) found that in ADHD, the brain networks responsible for goal-directed attention and cognitive control tend to be under-activated, while a different network - one linked to internally generated thoughts and mind-wandering, sometimes called the "default mode network" - tends to stay more active than it should during tasks that require focus. In plain terms: right when a student needs their brain's "get ready for the next thing" system to switch on, it's working harder than it should to switch off the "drifting" system. That's a real, measurable difference - not a motivation problem.

**Emotions aren't separate from this - they're part of it**

One of the more important recent shifts in ADHD research is recognizing emotional reactivity as a *core* feature of the condition, not an unrelated behavior problem layered on top. A major review (Shaw et al., 2014) found emotional dysregulation in an estimated 24-50% of children with ADHD, and a large, consistent difference in aggressive-type reactions compared to peers. So when a transition means walking away from something a student was enjoying, the emotional reaction that follows isn't a separate discipline issue - it's the ADHD showing up in a different form.

**What this means for how we respond**

None of this means transitions have to stay chaotic. It means the fix isn't "try harder" - it's reducing how much the transition demands all at once. The research on classroom interventions is actually very encouraging here.

The strongest evidence supports:

1. **Advance warnings paired with a visual cue.** A study on classroom transitions (Codding & Smyth, 2008) found that giving students a countdown *and* a visual timer - not just a verbal heads-up - reduced transition time and increased how engaged students were afterward. A separate study out of the University of Georgia used a projected visual cue with a shrinking timer and recovered nearly 40 extra minutes of instruction per week.

2. **Breaking multi-step transitions into short, explicit steps.** "Put away your reader, get your math folder, open to page 42" is three separate planning demands. Written or pictured as three separate steps, it's suddenly manageable.

3. **Consistent, rehearsed transition routines.** The same way you'd teach a new academic skill, transitions benefit from being explicitly taught and practiced - not just assumed.

4. **Simple reward or self-monitoring systems tied specifically to transitions.** A large meta-analysis of classroom interventions (Gaastra et al., 2016) found that reward-based (consequence) strategies and self-monitoring strategies produced the biggest improvements in on-task, non-disruptive behavior - bigger than most other approaches studied.

5. **Visual schedules.** These are worth using, and they help - but it's worth knowing that most of the rigorous research behind them comes from the autism research base rather than ADHD-specific trials. That doesn't mean they don't work for ADHD (the underlying logic - reducing uncertainty and memory load - applies just as much), but it's honest to say the ADHD-specific evidence is still catching up to how popular the strategy has become.

**A few myths worth retiring**

- *"They're being manipulative because they transition fine some days and not others."* Day-to-day variability is actually a documented feature of ADHD-related self-regulation, not evidence of choice.
- *"Older students should have outgrown this."* Research shows these executive-function differences persist into adolescence, and secondary students actually face *more* frequent, higher-stakes transitions (changing rooms and teachers) than younger children.
- *"Medication should handle this."* Medication reliably helps core symptoms, but research consistently shows its effect on organizational and academic-productivity outcomes is smaller and less reliable - which is exactly why classroom strategies matter even when a student is medicated.

**The bottom line**

Transitions are hard for ADHD students because they demand several mental skills simultaneously, in the domains where ADHD produces real, measurable differences - not because the student isn't trying. The good news is that the interventions with the strongest research support (advance warnings, visual timers, broken-down steps, and simple reward or self-monitoring systems) are also some of the most realistic to run in a normal classroom, with no special training required. Small changes to *how* a transition is structured can recover real instructional minutes - for that student, and often for the whole class.
